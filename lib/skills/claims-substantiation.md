# Claims Substantiation Skill

Campaign copy often contains claims that look harmless in a planning document but become legally significant the moment they appear in paid media, on a landing page, or in an email subject line. The substantiation checklist exists to flag these before they reach legal review, not after — because catching them late is expensive.

## Categories that always require review

**Time claims**: Any assertion about speed, duration, or immediacy. "In 30 minutes," "same-day," "overnight," "instant," "within 24 hours." These require operational evidence that the experience actually delivers on the stated timeframe, and they must be consistently true across all fulfilment scenarios.

**Sustainability claims**: "Eco-friendly," "sustainable," "carbon-neutral," "natural," "clean," "green," "planet-friendly," "responsibly sourced." Regulators in multiple markets (FTC in the US, ASA in the UK, EU Green Claims Directive) have tightened standards significantly. Each of these requires specific, substantiated evidence — not brand aspiration.

**Savings claims**: "Save X%," "up to X off," "best value," "lowest price," "more for less." These require a clear and accurate comparison basis. "Up to 50% off" must reflect actual reductions from a genuine prior price, not an inflated reference price.

**Health and wellness claims**: "Better sleep," "reduced stress," "improved focus," "supports immunity," "feel better." Depending on the product category and market, these may be regulated health claims requiring clinical evidence or regulatory pre-approval.

**Behavior-change claims**: "Helps you," "makes it easier to," "designed to," "proven to." These imply a mechanism of action. They need evidence that the product actually produces the stated behavioral change.

**Performance and superiority claims**: "Fastest," "most effective," "number one," "best in class," "outperforms." These require category-level evidence and clear definitions of what is being compared.

## What to include in the checklist

For each flagged claim, include:
- The claim category (e.g., "sustainability claim")
- The specific copy line or message that triggered the flag
- What substantiation would be required before it can be published

Don't clear claims — flag them for review. The role of this checklist is to surface, not to approve.

## Phrases to avoid in execution copy unless the brief provides proof

Do not write the following as if they are settled facts: "verified testimonials," "real customer testimonials," "customer names and photos," "user-generated content," "survey-backed," "proven," "validated by customers," "endorsements confirming benefits," "helps you regain your flow," "clear mental blocks," "no crash."

Use safer alternatives instead: "testimonial-style creative," "scenario-based proof," "influencer-style demonstration if contracted and approved," "customer proof if available," "validated testimonials if available," "claims requiring substantiation before publication," "positioned around perceived focus and refreshment," "supports an afternoon reset ritual."

Main execution copy should not sound final or already substantiated by default — that is the legal checklist's job, not the plan's.
