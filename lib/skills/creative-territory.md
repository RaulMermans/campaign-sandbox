# Creative Territory Skill

A campaign route is only useful if it's ownable — meaning another brand in the category couldn't run the same campaign without it feeling wrong. Generic route names are a symptom of generic thinking. The name is usually the first sign.

## Why route names matter

The name of a route does two jobs: it orients the creative team, and it survives deck reviews, client presentations, and production briefs. A name like "Effortless Elegance" does neither — it says nothing about what the campaign does, why it works, or what makes it specific to this brand. A name like "The 7PM Reset" or "The Hard Part, Handled" does both.

**Route names to avoid** (because they could apply to almost any brand in almost any premium or lifestyle category):
- Effortless Elegance, Urban Escape, Calm Curation, Premium Ritual, Simple Choice
- Elevated [Category], [Brand] Moments, The [Adjective] Journey, The Art of [X]
- Any name built on "effortless," "elevated," "curated," "quiet," or "pure" used as the primary identifier

When you find yourself writing one of these, ask: what specifically does this brand fight against, and what specifically does it give the audience? The answer to those two questions is usually the route name.

## Every route needs five things

**The enemy**: What attitude, behavior, or category convention does this route reject? Not a competitor brand — a cultural or behavioral villain the audience already dislikes. Naming the enemy makes the route's position concrete.

**The proof mechanism**: Why is this route believable? What does the brand actually do, show, or demonstrate that makes the claim credible — not just desirable? A proof mechanism is not a brand value. It's a specific execution choice.

**The visual world**: Describe what the campaign looks like in physical, specific terms: locations, lighting conditions, composition style, what objects are in frame, what is deliberately excluded. "Warm, intimate, authentic" is not a visual world. "A single overhead light in a home kitchen at 7pm, messy counter, one good pan, no food styling" is a visual world.

**The channel behavior**: How does this route move through media in a way that fits the creative idea? Some routes are inherently still and contemplative (long-form editorial, email, OOH). Others are inherently kinetic (TikTok, in-store). The channel should feel like a natural extension of the idea, not a media plan bolted on.

**The failure mode**: How does this route become embarrassing if the budget gets cut, the client gets nervous, or the production team plays it safe? Naming this in advance is how you protect the route in review.

## killerLine

The killerLine is not a tagline. It's the sharpest single expression of what this route believes — the line you'd put on a bus shelter or in an email subject line. It should make someone feel something specific, not just nod along. Test it: would a competitor's brand be able to say the same line without it feeling wrong? If yes, it's not specific enough.
